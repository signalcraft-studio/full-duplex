Goodbye GSM? The 2G and 3G switch-off around the world, explained

Published 15 October 2026

The networks that taught the world to text are being retired. Here's where things stand and why it matters for satellites.

The global picture

A July 2025 industry report filed with the ITU counts 131 operators in 65 countries and territories that have completed or planned 2G switch-offs, and 147 operators in 62 countries and territories that have completed, planned or are in progress with 3G switch-offs.[3] It notes the Orange group has mandated that 2G and 3G be switched off in Europe by 2030, though timing is likely to vary by country.[3]

The UK

EE and Vodafone have completed their 3G switch-offs, and Three has switched off 3G across most of the UK. O2 said it would follow in 2025.[1][2] All UK mobile network operators have told the Government they will switch off 2G by 2033 at the latest.[1] O2 said it would keep some 2G for several years, for example to carry emergency calls in remote areas without 4G, and would withdraw 2G and 3G access for inbound roamers from 1 October 2025.[2]

USA and Australia

According to Ofcom, all 3G networks in the USA have already been switched off.[1] In Australia, TPG Telecom switched off 3G in January 2024 and Telstra and Optus followed in late 2024.[4]

Why GSM is so sticky

2G hangs on because of the long tail: alarms, meters, payment terminals, older phones and roaming visitors. That is why switch-offs come with migration programmes rather than a single flick of a switch.

The satellite twist

Today's direct-to-cell services connect to 4G phones. Kyivstar, for example, says its Starlink service works with 4G (LTE) smartphones.[5] So one future scenario is a world where legacy 2G coverage in remote areas gives way to 4G on the ground plus satellite in the gaps. That's our analysis, not an announced plan.

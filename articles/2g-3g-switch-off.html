Goodbye GSM? The 2G and 3G switch-off around the world, explained

Who's switched off, who's next, and why 2G is so hard to kill.

By Sam Beer · 15 October 2026 · 2 min read

Share on X

The networks that taught the world to text are being retired. Here's where things stand and why it matters for satellites.

The global picture

A July 2025 industry report filed with the ITU counts 131 operators in 65 countries and territories that have completed or planned 2G switch-offs, and 147 operators in 62 countries and territories that have completed, planned or are in progress with 3G switch-offs.[3] It notes the Orange group has mandated that 2G and 3G be switched off in Europe by 2030, though timing is likely to vary by country.[3]

The UK

EE and Vodafone have completed their 3G switch-offs, and Three has switched off 3G across most of the UK. O2 said it would follow in 2025.[1][2] All UK mobile network operatorsMobile network operator A company that owns its own mobile network and spectrum licences, such as O2, EE or T-Mobile. Satellite firms partner with them to borrow their frequencies. Glossary have told the Government they will switch off 2G by 2033 at the latest.[1] O2 said it would keep some 2G for several years, for example to carry emergency calls in remote areas without 4G, and would withdraw 2G and 3G access for inbound roamers from 1 October 2025.[2]

USA and Australia

According to Ofcom, all 3G networks in the USA have already been switched off.[1] In Australia, TPG Telecom switched off 3G in January 2024 and Telstra and Optus followed in late 2024.[4]

Why GSM is so sticky

2G hangs on because of the long tail: alarms, meters, payment terminals, older phones and roamingRoaming Using another network when yours isn’t there, usually abroad. Satellite-to-phone works much like roaming: your phone attaches to a partner network that happens to be in space. Glossary visitors. That is why switch-offs come with migration programmes rather than a single flick of a switch.

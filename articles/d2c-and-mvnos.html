Where do MVNOs fit when phones connect to space?

Published 15 October 2026

Analysis from an MVNO insider. Between 2010 and 2016 I worked on the IVR and SMS workstream of Virgin Mobile's thin-to-thick MVNO programme (MTP), alongside EE, BT Wholesale and Huawei. Moving an MVNO from 'thin' (renting almost everything from the host network) to 'thick' (running more of its own core, messaging and customer platforms) teaches you one lesson: whoever controls the integration points controls the product.

How satellite plugs in today

Starlink describes its Direct to Cell service as integrating with operators 'similar to a standard roaming partner'.[1] The deals are struck with network operators who own the spectrum: VMO2 in the UK uses its own licensed spectrum transmitted from space.[2] That puts host networks in the driving seat.

Who might get access

Not every model runs through the big networks. Skylo reports around 40 partners, including MVNOs as well as operators, device makers and chipset suppliers.[3] In the US, T-Mobile, AT&T and Verizon plan a joint venture to pool spectrum and create a single platform for satellite operators to connect to.[4]

Questions MVNOs should be asking

  • Wholesale terms: will satellite fallback be included in host agreements, or sold as an add-on?
  • Messaging and IVR: if a customer texts via satellite, do your SMS platform, short codes and service messages still work? On a thick MVNO, that's partly your stack.
  • Emergency and support: how will your contact centre and IVR explain 'SAT' mode to a customer stuck on a hillside?
  • Pricing: VMO2 charges £3 a month on Pay Monthly plans.[5] Can an MVNO match that price, or use satellite to stand out from rivals?

My view: satellite fallback will become a standard feature, and T-Mobile's CEO has called it 'table stakes'.[6] MVNOs that don't secure it in their wholesale deals risk looking like they have less coverage.

Why it has to be low

A phone transmits with tiny power through a small antenna. To hear it, satellites fly in low Earth orbitLEO Low Earth orbit: roughly 500 to 2,000 km up. Close enough for a phone’s weak signal to reach, but each satellite crosses the sky in minutes, so you need hundreds of them. Glossary and use large, high-gain phased-arrayPhased array A flat antenna made of many small elements whose timing is adjusted to steer the beam electronically, with no moving parts. AST’s BlueBirds unfold huge ones. Glossary antennas.[2] Long delays and random-access problems also restrict this design to low orbits.[1]

The Doppler problem

A low-orbit satellite moves at around 7.5 km per second at 600 km altitude, producing a Doppler shift of tens of kilohertz, and stays in view for only a few minutes.[4] In today's direct-to-cell systems the network does the correcting, pre-compensating downlink Doppler and post-compensating uplink, so existing phones work unchanged.[1] In the standardised 3GPP3GPP The body that writes the global mobile standards (GSM, 3G, 4G LTE, 5G). Its Release 17 added satellite (NTN) support to the standards in 2022. Glossary 5G 'NTNNTN Non-terrestrial network: the official 3GPP term for mobile networks that use satellites (or high-altitude platforms) instead of, or as well as, masts on the ground. Glossary' approach, the phone helps out, using GNSS and satellite orbit data to pre-compensate itself.[5]

Why it's texts first

Bandwidth per beam is limited, so operators start with messaging. Kyivstar says it began with SMS 'due to the limited bandwidth of satellite channels', with voice and data to follow.[6]

What you'll notice

You'll usually need to be outdoors with a view of the sky, and services start with texts and selected apps.[6][7]

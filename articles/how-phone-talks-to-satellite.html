How does an ordinary phone talk to a satellite? A plain-English guide

Published 15 October 2026

A cell tower in the sky

In direct-to-cell systems, the satellite carries base-station functions (an LTE 'eNodeB'), so an unmodified smartphone connects using familiar protocols, much as it would to a distant mast. Traffic then goes down to ground stations or across inter-satellite links.[1] The satellite borrows the mobile operator's own spectrum, which is why your phone roams onto it automatically.[2] In the UK, for example, O2 Satellite uses O2's licensed 1800 MHz spectrum.[3]

Why it has to be low

A phone transmits with tiny power through a small antenna. To hear it, satellites fly in low Earth orbit and use large, high-gain phased-array antennas.[2] Long delays and random-access problems also restrict this design to low orbits.[1]

The Doppler problem

A low-orbit satellite moves at around 7.5 km per second at 600 km altitude, producing a Doppler shift of tens of kilohertz, and stays in view for only a few minutes.[4] In today's direct-to-cell systems the network does the correcting, pre-compensating downlink Doppler and post-compensating uplink, so existing phones work unchanged.[1] In the standardised 3GPP 5G 'NTN' approach, the phone helps out, using GNSS and satellite orbit data to pre-compensate itself.[5]

Why it's texts first

Bandwidth per beam is limited, so operators start with messaging. Kyivstar says it began with SMS 'due to the limited bandwidth of satellite channels', with voice and data to follow.[6]

What you'll notice

You'll usually need to be outdoors with a view of the sky, and services start with texts and selected apps.[6][7]

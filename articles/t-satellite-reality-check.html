T-Satellite a year on: lifeline, not a replacement

What the US tells us about how much people will actually use it.

By Sam Beer · 15 October 2026 · 1 min read

Share on X

T-Mobile launched T-Satellite with Starlink commercially in July 2025. It works on most modern smartphones and covers parts of the US with no cell towers.[1] T-Mobile says more than 150,000 people use it every day, and it now carries data for a set of satellite-ready apps including WhatsApp, Google Maps and AllTrails.[1]

Where it proved itself: disasters

T-Mobile says T-Satellite supported more than 250,000 users and 1.5 million messages during Winter Storm Fern, and delivered more than 100 Wireless Emergency Alerts. It is also expanding through reciprocal roamingRoaming Using another network when yours isn’t there, usually abroad. Satellite-to-phone works much like roaming: your phone attaches to a partner network that happens to be in space. Glossary in Canada and New Zealand.[2]

Where it hasn't (yet): everyday traffic

CEO Srini Gopalan has said satellite accounts for about 0.0002% of T-Mobile's network usage, and only 0.0003% in peak summer months. He calls it a 'complementary' service that will become 'table stakes'.[3] He also confirmed that the exclusivity deal with Starlink does not end in 2026. T-Mobile is working on a D2DD2D Direct to device: a satellite talking straight to an ordinary phone, with no dish or special handset. Your phone thinks it has found another mast, just one a few hundred kilometres up. Glossary joint venture with AT&T and Verizon.[3][4]

The Full Duplex view

Direct-to-cellDirect to cell Starlink’s name (and the industry’s shorthand, D2C) for satellites that act as a mobile mast in space and connect to normal 4G phones through a partner network. Glossary is becoming a standard safety net, much as roaming once did. The bigger question is who controls the satellite layer, and that is what the carrier joint venture is about.

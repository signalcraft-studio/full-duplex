Where phones are really talking to satellites: what Opensignal's new map shows

Real-world D2D connections, who leads, and why Europe is still quiet.

By Sam Beer · 19 October 2026 · 4 min read

Share on X

Satellite-to-phone launches get announced at a steady pace. Opensignal's D2D Satellite Map, launched on 12 October, is one of the first public attempts to show where phones are actually using them.[1][3] Each point is a place where a smartphone connected to a direct-to-device (D2D) satellite service at some point since 11 January 2026.[2] Opensignal says the map will be updated monthly.[2] The version online now uses data up to 20 September 2026.[1]

Who leads

Activity is concentrated in a few countries. According to Opensignal staff speaking to PCMag and Fierce Network, the map shows heavy use in the United States, with Australia, Canada, Chile, Japan, Peru and New Zealand also clearly lit, while most of the rest of the world is dark or nearly so.[4][5] Opensignal told PCMag and Fierce Network that more than 90% of the connections it detects are on Starlink.[4][5]

These are largely the markets where Starlink's operator partners went live early: T-Mobile in the US, Rogers in Canada, Entel in Chile and Peru, KDDI in Japan, One NZ and Telstra.[14]–[19] That's a pattern, not proof. The map doesn't show which network each phone was on.

Why Europe and Africa are quiet

This part is my analysis, not Opensignal's. The simplest explanation is time. Europe's first commercial service, O2 Satellite, only went live on 26 February 2026, the day after Ofcom's rules letting handsets connect took effect.[7][9] Elsewhere in Europe, most of what exists is still trials. Orange España has a pilot in Valladolid, and Salt in Switzerland is waiting for Swiss approvals.[11][12] VodafoneThree's UK beta is now aimed at early 2027.[10]

In Africa, Starlink phone launches are very recent. Airtel Uganda went live on 24 September, four days after the current data cut-off, so it can't show up yet.[13]

Two other things hold the numbers down everywhere. First, Opensignal told PCMag the map only uses data from Android phones, collected through its own app and partner apps, because Apple doesn't give third-party apps enough access.[4] Second, D2D is a fallback. O2, for one, connects phones to the satellite only when there's no mobile signal, so a country with good coverage will show less use even if the service is live.[7]

The scattered dots in countries with no service have an explanation too. Opensignal's FAQ says these are mostly edge cases, such as people roaming from a country that has a satellite plan.[2]

What it means if you're on O2 Satellite

The UK is on the map, but only lightly. ISPreview's look at the UK found O2 Satellite connections mostly in rural areas and along roads with patchy coverage, including a long stretch of the A34.[6]

Three things are worth knowing:

  • It isn't a coverage map. A dot means a phone connected there. A gap doesn't mean O2 Satellite won't work there. O2 says the service took its UK landmass coverage from 89% to 95%.[7]
  • iPhone use isn't counted. O2 Satellite has worked on iPhone since 28 May, but none of that use appears on the map.[4][8]
  • A connection isn't a message. Opensignal's Lian Carl told Fierce Network that a dot shows the phone reached a satellite, not that a text or any data was sent.[5]

Opensignal says it plans to add how well these connections perform, not just where they happen.[3] That will be the version that matters most to O2 customers, because it will show whether a WhatsApp message actually gets through from a Welsh hillside. Until then, treat the map as a picture of where satellite fallback is being used, not a guide to where it works.

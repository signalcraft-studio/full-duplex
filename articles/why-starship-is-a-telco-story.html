Why Starship is a telco story

Launch cadence and next-gen sats decide when phone-to-satellite coverage goes continuous.

By Sam Beer · 25 October 2026 · 5 min read

Share on X

Direct-to-cell lives or dies on one boring question: how fast can you put enough capable satellites over people's heads? That is why Starship belongs in a mobile briefing, not only a space one.

What "continuous" actually means for a phone

A low-orbit satellite stays in view for only a few minutes, and it is moving at roughly 7 km/s.[1] For a phone user, "coverage" means there is almost always another satellite ready to take over, with spare capacity in the beam. Sparse fleets create windows: texts and a few apps when the sky is clear, then nothing. Dense fleets start to feel like ordinary mobile fallback.

Today's first-generation Starlink Direct to Cell service already runs on more than 650 dedicated satellites and reaches unmodified 4G phones outdoors.[2] That is enough for a useful safety net in partner markets, but not the always-on, city-grade experience carriers eventually want to sell. The next step is not a slogan. It is more satellites, with more radio power and more beams, flown often enough that gaps close.

Why bigger satellites help tiny phones

The bottleneck is the handset. A phone has a small antenna and little uplink power, so the satellite has to do the heavy lifting with large phased arrays and smart beamforming.[1] Put simply: more aperture and more spectrum at the satellite end buy more capacity per user on the ground.

That is the point of SpaceX's next-generation Starlink Mobile (V2) constellation. In early October 2026 the FCC authorised SpaceX to deploy up to 15,000 of these direct-to-device satellites in very low orbits between 326 and 335 km, with half to be in assigned orbits by 7 October 2032 and the full authorised fleet by 7 October 2035.[2][3][5] Light Reading reports SpaceX is targeting user speeds up to about 150 Mbit/s on V2, up from about 4 Mbit/s on the current generation; SpaceX itself says the V2 mobiles will deliver more than 100 times the bandwidth of today's Direct to Cell system.[2][4]

Those figures are targets, not a promise that your phone will stream HD from a hillside tomorrow. But the direction of travel is clear: without a step-change in satellite capability, direct-to-cell stays a messaging and light-data add-on.

Launch cadence is the real buildout plan

Authorising 15,000 satellites is not the same as flying them. Light Reading notes SpaceX will lean on Starship to deliver the larger D2D satellites into orbit in volume.[2] SpaceX has said the same in its own updates: Starship's payload capacity is part of how the next Direct to Cell generation gets built, alongside exclusive spectrum and new phased arrays.[4]

Starship reached orbit on its 28 September 2026 test flight; the vehicle is still proving reuse and cadence, not yet a routine airliner schedule.[2] For carriers and customers, that matters more than any launch-day livestream. Every quarter Starship slips is a quarter the denser, higher-capacity phone constellation stays on paper — which delays when "open sky" stops meaning "maybe later this hour".

The FCC's 2032 / 2035 milestones set a regulatory clock.[2][3] Physics and manufacturing set a harder one: how many next-gen mobiles leave the factory, and how often a rocket big enough to carry them actually flies.

What this does to carrier timelines

Virgin Media O2 / T-Mobile and other Starlink partners. O2 Satellite is already live in the UK on Starlink Direct to Cell over O2's 1800 MHz spectrum; T-Mobile's T-Satellite has been commercial in the US since mid-2025.[6][7] Those services ride the first-generation fleet. The experience upgrade customers will notice — more apps, more reliable sessions, fewer "try again when a bird is overhead" moments — tracks the V2 buildout and Starship's ability to loft it. Spectrum deals and handset support still matter; without launch rate they are paperwork.

VodafoneThree and AST SpaceMobile. The other UK horse is not waiting on Starship. It is waiting on AST's BlueBird fleet. Vodafone says UK beta testing needs about 45 AST satellites, now expected at the beginning of 2027 after earlier summer-2026 trial talk slipped.[8] AST's Block 2 BlueBirds are large (about 2,400 square feet of array) and only a handful fit per rocket — New Glenn is designed for up to eight per mission — so a failed or delayed launch immediately moves partner dates.[9][10] Same industry constraint, different rocket. See our UK D2D race and O2 Satellite pieces for how the two UK paths compare.

In short: Starlink partners inherit SpaceX's launch cadence. AST partners inherit whoever is flying BlueBirds that month. Continuous phone coverage is a logistics problem dressed as a network one.

What to watch next

  • First operational batches of V2 Starlink Mobile satellites, and whether Starship is the vehicle that actually flies them at scale.[2][4]
  • Whether Starship flight rate moves from test shots to a repeatable cadence — that, more than any single demo, gates when high-bandwidth direct-to-cell can feel continuous.
  • AST's path to ~45 satellites for early-2027 partner betas, including New Glenn and Falcon manifests.[8][10]
  • Our direct-to-cell tracker for which markets are live, trialling or still announced-only as those fleets fill in.

Starship will keep making headlines for reasons that have nothing to do with your SIM. For Full Duplex, the telco story is narrower and more useful: launch cost and cadence decide when a normal phone stops treating the sky as a sometimes-network.

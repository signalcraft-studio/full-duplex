Glossary

Satellite-to-phone jargon, in plain English

The terms you’ll meet in our articles and explainers. Tap or hover a dotted word anywhere on the site to see its meaning without leaving the page.

Definitions by a mobile network insider with over 13 years in GSM. Checked 13 November 2026.

3GPP

The body that writes the global mobile standards (GSM, 3G, 4G LTE, 5G). Its Release 17 added satellite (NTN) support to the standards in 2022.

Source: 3GPP: Non-Terrestrial Networks (NTN) overview

Backhaul

The link that carries traffic from a mast back to the core network. Satellites such as Starlink broadband are often used as backhaul for remote masts, which is different from talking to phones.

Source: Wikipedia: Backhaul

Bent pipe

A satellite that just relays: it takes the signal from your phone, shifts its frequency, amplifies it and beams it down to a ground station, with no processing on board. 3GPP calls it a transparent payload.

Source: 3GPP: Non-Terrestrial Networks (NTN) overview

Constellation

A fleet of satellites working together, spread across orbits so that at least one is always overhead.

Source: Wikipedia: Satellite constellation

D2D

Direct to device: a satellite talking straight to an ordinary phone, with no dish or special handset. Your phone thinks it has found another mast, just one a few hundred kilometres up.

Source: 3GPP: Non-Terrestrial Networks (NTN) overview

Direct to cell

Starlink’s name (and the industry’s shorthand, D2C) for satellites that act as a mobile mast in space and connect to normal 4G phones through a partner network.

Source: 3GPP: Non-Terrestrial Networks (NTN) overview

eNodeB

The 3GPP name for a 4G base station (the 5G one is a gNodeB): the radio kit at the top of a mast. A direct-to-cell satellite does this job from space.

Source: 3GPP TS 36.300 (E-UTRAN overall description)

eSIM

A SIM built into the phone that you download instead of inserting. Handy for adding a second line, such as a satellite plan, without swapping cards.

Source: GSMA: eSIM

Frequency band

A numbered block of mobile spectrum defined by 3GPP, such as band 25 (1900 MHz). Your phone has to support the band a satellite uses for the satellite to reach it.

Source: 3GPP: LTE

GEO

Geostationary orbit: 35,786 km above the equator, where a satellite seems to hang still in the sky. Great for TV, but too far for an ordinary phone to talk to directly.

Source: 3GPP: Non-Terrestrial Networks (NTN) overview

Ground station

A dish site on Earth that links satellites to the internet and to the mobile network’s core. With a bent-pipe satellite, your call or message comes down here before it goes anywhere else.

Source: 3GPP: Non-Terrestrial Networks (NTN) overview

GSM

Global System for Mobile communications: the 2G standard from 1991 that started it all, and the base that SMS, roaming and SIM cards were built on.

Source: Wikipedia: GSM

Handover

Passing a connection from one cell to another without dropping it. Handing over between a ground mast and a satellite is one of the harder problems in direct to device.

Source: Wikipedia: Handover

IMEI

Your phone’s unique serial number (dial *#06# to see it). Networks use it to check which features a handset supports. Our checker never asks for it.

Source: Wikipedia: IMEI

LEO

Low Earth orbit: roughly 500 to 2,000 km up. Close enough for a phone’s weak signal to reach, but each satellite crosses the sky in minutes, so you need hundreds of them.

Source: 3GPP: Non-Terrestrial Networks (NTN) overview

Link budget

The sum of every gain and loss between a transmitter and a receiver. A phone has a tiny antenna and little power, so a satellite needs a big antenna and a low orbit to close the link budget.

Source: 3GPP: Non-Terrestrial Networks (NTN) overview · Wikipedia: Link budget

LTE

Long Term Evolution, the standard behind 4G. Starlink’s direct-to-cell service speaks LTE, which is why it works with phones people already own.

Source: 3GPP: LTE

MHz

Megahertz, a million cycles a second: how radio frequencies are measured. Lower frequencies (say 700 MHz) travel further and get through better; higher ones carry more data.

Source: Wikipedia: Radio spectrum

Mobile network operator

A company that owns its own mobile network and spectrum licences, such as O2, EE or T-Mobile. Satellite firms partner with them to borrow their frequencies.

Source: Wikipedia: Mobile network operator

MSS

Mobile-satellite service: radio spectrum set aside internationally for satellites that talk to mobile terminals. Globalstar, Skylo’s partners and others use MSS bands rather than a mobile network’s own frequencies.

Source: Wikipedia: Mobile-satellite service

MVNO

Mobile virtual network operator: a brand that sells mobile service over someone else’s network. A thin MVNO mostly resells; a thick MVNO runs its own core network, SIMs and billing.

Source: Wikipedia: Mobile virtual network operator

NTN

Non-terrestrial network: the official 3GPP term for mobile networks that use satellites (or high-altitude platforms) instead of, or as well as, masts on the ground.

Source: 3GPP: Non-Terrestrial Networks (NTN) overview

Phased array

A flat antenna made of many small elements whose timing is adjusted to steer the beam electronically, with no moving parts. AST’s BlueBirds unfold huge ones.

Source: Wikipedia: Phased array

RCS

Rich Communication Services: the upgrade to SMS that adds read receipts, typing indicators and photos, used by Google Messages and, since iOS 18, iPhone.

Source: GSMA: RCS

Roaming

Using another network when yours isn’t there, usually abroad. Satellite-to-phone works much like roaming: your phone attaches to a partner network that happens to be in space.

Source: Wikipedia: Roaming

SCS

Supplemental Coverage from Space: the US FCC’s rules (2024) that let a satellite operator use a mobile network’s spectrum to fill its coverage gaps, as Starlink does with T-Mobile.

Source: FCC: Supplemental Coverage from Space framework (14 Mar 2024)

Spectrum

The range of radio frequencies. Governments license slices of it, and who owns which slice decides who can talk to your phone, which is why satellite firms team up with mobile networks.

Source: Wikipedia: Radio spectrum

Inner Signal: the life of a phone call

A grandmother in Warminster rings her grandson’s mobile from her landline. He is hiking in the Highlands, miles from the nearest mast. We shrink down and ride the call, from the moment she lifts the handset to the line on her bill.

Written by a mobile network insider with over 13 years in GSM, from IVR and SMS to call routing and thin and thick MVNOs. Preview build

An honest note. In the UK today, satellite-to-phone means texts and some apps. Voice calls are the next step. The satellite leg of this story shows the near future being built.

Start the journey ↓

Meet the cast

  • The secret agent

    IMSI

    Lives in the SIM and in the network’s HLR vault. It is your real identity, and it almost never travels over the air. Over the air he wears a disguise mask, the TMSI, which the network swaps regularly so nobody can follow him.

  • The ship

    IMEI

    The handset itself, with a unique 15-digit hull number stamped on it. Border control (the EIR) checks it against the stolen-phone list.

  • The key that never leaves

    Ki

    A glowing secret key locked inside the SIM, with a twin locked in the network’s AuC vault. They are never in the same room, which is exactly why the trick works.

  • The public name tag

    MSISDN

    The phone number everyone knows. It is only a label, and the network translates it into the IMSI behind the scenes.

  • Our shrunken craft

    The Call Pod

    It carries Gran’s voice from the moment she lifts the handset to the line on her bill.

Every number in this story is made up. Phone numbers, IMSI and IMEI values are examples, and 07700 900xxx is a range set aside for drama. Names and message formats are the real ones.

Chapter 1 of 14 · Landline network

Gran picks up the phone

In a kitchen in Warminster, Gran lifts the handset. That closes an electrical circuit on her phone line, and the exchange down the road notices straight away. It plays a dial tone, which really means “I’m listening”. She taps in her grandson’s mobile number, and we shrink down and climb aboard the Call Pod.

  • Call Pod

Analogue line signalling

Handset lifted  →  line loop closes, exchange detects off-hook
Exchange  →  dial tone: 350 Hz + 450 Hz (UK)
Gran dials 07700 900123  →  DTMF, two tones per digit
   “7” = 852 Hz + 1209 Hz     “0” = 941 Hz + 1336 Hz
  • Tone dialling (DTMF) sends each digit as two pitches at once, so the exchange can tell them apart from speech.
  • UK landlines are moving off the old copper phone network (the PSTN) and onto broadband. Openreach says that happens on 31 January 2027. The story below is the classic version, and the call still ends up in the same place.

Chapter 2 of 14 · Landline network

The exchange hands it on

Gran’s exchange sees a number starting 07. That means mobile, and it isn’t one of its own, so it passes the call up to a bigger “transit” exchange. That works out which mobile operator looks after the number (people can switch network and keep their number, so it has to check) and hands the call over at the operator’s front door, the Gateway MSC. The message that does the handing over is called an IAM.

  • Call Pod

SS7 · ISUP

ISUP  IAM  (Initial Address Message)
   Called party number  : +44 7700 900123
   Calling party number : Gran’s landline (presentation allowed)
   Transmission medium  : speech
   → sent at a Point of Interconnect to the mobile operator’s Gateway MSC
  • ISUP is the call-setup language of the old telephone network. It runs over SS7 signalling links, and today often over IP as SIP-I.
  • MSC means Mobile Switching Centre: the exchange of a mobile network. The “Gateway” one is the one that takes calls arriving from other networks.

Chapter 3 of 14 · Mobile core

“Where is he?”

A Nokia 3310, still in the archive. It would like you to know it also holds a very good game of Snake.

The Gateway MSC has a phone number but no idea where the phone is. It asks the HLR, the network’s master register of subscribers. The HLR takes the public name tag (the MSISDN, which is simply the phone number) and finds the secret agent behind it: the IMSI, the identity that lives in the SIM. The number everyone knows is not who you really are.

  • MSISDN
  • IMSI

SS7 · MAP

GMSC → HLR   MAP  SendRoutingInfo
   msisdn            : 447700900123
   interrogationType : basicCall
HLR: look up MSISDN → IMSI  001 01 0123456789  (illustrative)
HLR: known? barred? diverts set? which VLR is he registered in?
  • In 4G and 5G the HLR’s job is done by the HSS and UDM. Same idea: a very large database that says who you are and where you are.
  • IMSI = 15 digits: country code (234 for the UK), network code, then your personal number. The IMSI in these examples is from the ITU test range, not a real customer.

Chapter 4 of 14 · Mobile core

A room key for one call

The HLR knows which area his phone last reported in, but that is not a number you can ring. So it asks the visitor register there (the VLR) for a temporary routing number made for this one call, the MSRN. Think of a hotel room key cut for a single stay: it works once, then it is useless. The HLR passes it back, and the Gateway MSC sends the call on towards it.

  • IMSI
  • MSISDN

SS7 · MAP

HLR → VLR   MAP  ProvideRoamingNumber
   imsi, msc-Number, msisdn
VLR → HLR   result: roamingNumber (MSRN)   (from the serving MSC’s own number pool)
HLR → GMSC   SendRoutingInfo result: MSRN
GMSC → serving MSC   ISUP  IAM  to the MSRN
  • The VLR is the network’s short-term memory of who is currently in its area. It also holds the TMSI mask that goes with each IMSI.
  • If he had set “divert all calls”, the HLR would have returned the diversion number instead of an MSRN.

Chapter 5 of 14 · Space

The twist: there is no mast

Here is the clever bit. The grandson is in a glen with no mobile signal, but a satellite overhead is acting as a mobile mast in space. The Call Pod rides a beam from a ground station (the gateway) up to the satellite, which relays it down to his phone on ordinary mobile frequencies. His phone does not know it is not talking to a mast.

An honest note. In the UK today, satellite-to-phone means texts and some apps. Voice calls are the next step. This leg of the story shows the near future being built, not something you can dial today.

  • Call Pod

Satellite radio

Mobile core ↔ Ground gateway     fibre
Ground gateway ↔ Satellite        FEEDER LINK: radio link between satellite and ground
Satellite ↔ His phone             SERVICE LINK: mobile-band spectrum, standard handset
  • Some designs put the base station inside the satellite. Others keep it on the ground and use the satellite as a relay. Either way the phone sees a normal cell.
  • Real satellite-to-phone systems are 4G or 5G based (the 3GPP “NTN” standards), so voice would run as VoLTE rather than through an MSC. We draw the classic GSM path because it is the clearest way to see who does what.

Chapter 6 of 14 · Space

Shouting his disguise across the glen

Now the network has to find him. It calls out his temporary disguise, the TMSI, across the whole satellite beam, which is far bigger than a normal mobile cell. A disguise matters: shouting his real identity over a wide area would let anyone with a radio follow him around. Only one phone in the glen recognises the mask, and it answers.

  • IMSI
  • TMSI mask

BSSMAP · radio paging

MSC/VLR → radio network   BSSMAP  PAGING  (tmsi 0xA1B2C3D4, location area)
Radio network → air          Paging Request on the paging channel, every cell in the area
Phone → network              Channel Request, then Paging Response (TMSI)
  • The TMSI is handed out by the VLR and changed regularly, so one hidden number cannot be followed from place to place.
  • Paging a big beam costs a lot of radio capacity, which is one reason satellite networks are careful about how often they do it.

Chapter 7 of 14 · Space

“Prove you’re you”

Before connecting, the network checks the agent is genuine without ever asking for his secret. It sends a random number, the RAND. Inside the SIM, the glowing key (Ki) scrambles it into an answer (SRES). Back in the network’s AuC vault, the twin key has already worked out the right answer. If they match, the agent is real. Both sides also make a one-off ciphering key (Kc) that scrambles the call. Ki itself never leaves either vault.

  • IMSI
  • Ki
  • Ki’s twin (AuC)

GSM security · DTAP

AuC (in advance)  RAND → A3(Ki) = SRES,  A8(Ki) = Kc   →  triplet (RAND, SRES, Kc) to the VLR
VLR → phone   DTAP  Authentication Request   RAND (128 bit)
SIM           SRES′ = A3(Ki, RAND)  (32 bit);   Kc = A8(Ki, RAND)  (64 bit)
phone → VLR   Authentication Response   SRES′
VLR           SRES′ = SRES ?   yes →  Cipher Mode Command (A5 encryption, key Kc)
  • A3 and A8 are the operator’s choice of algorithm. Newer networks (3G, 4G, 5G) also make the network prove itself to the phone. Original GSM only checked the phone.
  • The AuC usually works the answers out ahead of time and hands the VLR a ready-made “triplet”, so the vault does not need to be woken for every call.

Chapter 8 of 14 · Mobile core

Border control for the ship

The agent checks out, but what about the ship he is travelling in? Every phone has a unique 15-digit serial number, the IMEI, stamped on its hull. The network asks the EIR, the border-control register, whether that number has been reported stolen. Green light: cleared to proceed.

  • IMEI
  • EIR

SS7 · MAP

MSC → phone   DTAP  Identity Request (IMEI)
phone → MSC   Identity Response   IMEI 35 123456 654321 1   (illustrative)
MSC → EIR     MAP  CheckIMEI (imei)
EIR → MSC     equipmentStatus = whiteListed
  • The first 8 digits say which make and model (the TAC), the next 6 are the serial number, and the last is a check digit.
  • Stolen phones are added to a shared block list, so a phone reported stolen on one UK network can be blocked on all of them.

Chapter 9 of 14 · Mobile core

The bouncer at the bar

His SIM is prepaid, so before his phone rings the operator’s charging system is asked: is this call allowed, and is there credit? Receiving a call on your home network is usually free in the UK, but the same check guards roaming and satellite services, which can cost money. The question travels over CAMEL, a rulebook that lets an operator’s prepaid platform step into any call. Credit is fine, so the bouncer waves us through.

  • IMSI

CAMEL · CAP

GMSC → SCP   CAP  InitialDP
   serviceKey, calledPartyNumber, imsi, eventTypeBCSM = termAttemptAuthorized
SCP → charging   balance and rate check
SCP → GMSC   CAP  Continue     (or Connect to change the number, ReleaseCall to refuse,
                                 ApplyCharging to set a time limit)
  • In a live network the Gateway MSC often asks this earlier, before the phone is paged. We tell it here so the “is he allowed?” checks sit together just before the ring.
  • SCP stands for Service Control Point, the “brain” that runs prepaid and other intelligent-network rules. This is where a lot of real operator money is made and lost.

Chapter 10 of 14 · Landline network

Ring ring: the meter starts

Meter 4:12 call connected, charging

His phone buzzes in his pocket. A message called ACM (Address Complete) races all the way back to Gran’s exchange, which plays her the ringing tone. He answers with a cheery “hello?”, and a second message, ANM (Answer), tells every exchange along the route that the call is connected. At that moment, the meter starts.

  • Call Pod

ISUP · DTAP

phone → MSC   DTAP  Alerting         →  ISUP  ACM  back to Warminster  →  ringback tone
phone → MSC   DTAP  Connect          →  ISUP  ANM  →  charging starts
SCP           CAP  EventReportBCSM (tAnswer)  →  prepaid timer begins
  • No ANM means no billing. Until someone answers, the call is free, which is why a lot of fraud tries to get calls “answered” without a person.

Chapter 11 of 14 · Space

Her voice, in disguise

Gran’s line: G.71164 kbit/s

Mobile: AMR12.2 kbit/s

On Gran’s side, her voice is digital audio at 64 thousand bits a second (G.711, the telephone network’s standard since the 1970s). That is too bulky for a radio link, so a media gateway squeezes it into a mobile format called AMR, using a fifth of the space or less. Up it goes to the satellite, which is travelling at about 17,000 mph, so the network constantly adjusts timing and frequency to cancel the Doppler effect: the same trick that makes an ambulance siren change pitch as it passes.

  • Call Pod

G.711 · AMR · radio

Gran’s side    G.711 A-law PCM   8 kHz × 8 bit = 64 kbit/s
Media gateway  transcode G.711 → AMR   (AMR-NB 12.2 kbit/s, or AMR-WB up to 23.85 kbit/s)
Satellite      low orbit ~550 km, speed ≈ 7.6 km/s ≈ 17,000 mph
Radio          Doppler shift of tens of kHz at ~2 GHz is pre-compensated;
               timing advance is corrected for the long distance (3GPP NTN, Release 17)
  • 12.2 kbit/s is about 19% of 64 kbit/s. Newer wideband codecs sound better and still use far less than the old standard.
  • Standard GSM timing only copes with distances up to about 35 km. Satellite systems need extra timing tricks, which is part of what the NTN standards add.

Chapter 12 of 14 · Landline network

Hang-up: the lights go out

When Gran says goodbye and puts the phone down, the end of the call travels the same road. A REL (Release) message goes hop by hop along the route, and each exchange answers RLC (Release Complete) once it has let go of the line. Behind the pod the lights go out, one stop at a time. The radio channel is handed back, the beam moves on to other people, and the voice path ceases to exist.

  • Call Pod

SS7 · ISUP · DTAP

Gran hangs up  →  ISUP  REL  (cause 16: normal call clearing)  hop by hop
Each switch   →  ISUP  RLC  once its circuit is freed
Radio side    →  DTAP  Disconnect, Release, Release Complete;  radio channel freed
SCP           →  CAP  EventReportBCSM (tDisconnect), final charge reported
  • “Cause 16” is the polite code. Others tell you the number was busy, unallocated or that the network ran out of capacity.

Chapter 13 of 14 · Back office

The afterlife: a line on a bill

Gran’s bill07700 9001234 min 12s

A call does not really end when you hang up. The switch writes a small receipt, a CDR (call detail record). It drifts to mediation, which tidies and merges receipts from lots of machines. Rating puts a price on it, billing puts it on an invoice, and the operators settle up with each other, because Gran’s operator pays the mobile operator a small fee for delivering the call. The grave: one line on Gran’s bill.

  • IMSI
  • IMEI

Billing · settlement

MSC/GMSC  →  CDR   (A and B numbers, start and end time, duration 252 s, cause, IMSI, IMEI, cell)
Mediation   collect, validate, remove duplicates, join records, convert to billing format
Rating      252 s × Gran’s tariff;   wholesale: mobile termination charge
Billing     invoice line:   07700 900123    4 min 12s
Settlement  originating operator pays the terminating operator, usually netted off in wholesale billing
  • For prepaid there is no monthly bill. The record feeds the charging system that already took the money while you talked.
  • 07700 900xxx is a range Ofcom sets aside for TV and drama, so no real person will ever answer it.

Alternate ending: nobody answers

Tech support desk, circa 2007. “Have you tried turning it off and on again?” An in-joke from the author’s days on the support floor.

In another universe the grandson is deep in a peat bog with his phone in his bag, and does not answer. After 20 to 30 seconds of ringing, the network’s no-reply timer runs out and the call is quietly diverted to voicemail. A friendly robot voice (the IVR) asks Gran to leave a message after the tone. If his phone had been out of reach altogether, the network would have diverted her at once.

  • Call Pod

ISUP · call forwarding

GMSC/MSC   no-reply timer (NRy, typically 20 to 30 s) expires
HLR profile  call forwarding on no reply → voicemail access number
ISUP IAM     redirecting number = his number, redirection reason = no reply  →  voicemail platform
IVR          plays greeting, records message
Voicemail    SMS to him: “You have a new voicemail”   (MAP MT-ForwardSM)
  • The “not reachable” case (switched off, no signal) is a separate setting and diverts immediately, so Gran never hears a ring.
  • IVR means Interactive Voice Response: any computer that talks to callers and listens to their key presses or voice.

The whole trip in one breath

One tap of a landline, a dozen different computers, two radio links, one satellite moving at 17,000 mph and a billing system that would not give up. It all took a few seconds to connect and left a single line on a bill: 07700 900123, 4 min 12s.

Satellite voice calls to ordinary phones are not here in the UK yet, but the pieces in this story are being built right now. Signal is where we follow them.

Where the details come from
  • Call setup and routing: ITU-T Q.763 (ISUP messages), 3GPP TS 23.018 (basic call handling), 3GPP TS 29.002 (MAP).
  • Prepaid and CAMEL: 3GPP TS 23.078 and TS 29.078 (CAP).
  • Numbers and identities (IMSI, TMSI, IMEI, MSRN): 3GPP TS 23.003.
  • GSM security (RAND, SRES, Kc, A3, A8, A5): 3GPP TS 43.020.
  • Satellite access to ordinary phones: 3GPP NTN work, Release 17 onwards.
  • UK landline switch-off: Openreach, Big Switch Off.
